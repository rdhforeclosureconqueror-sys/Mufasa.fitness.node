## Story

When the storm came, it did not ask which families were speaking to one another.

It tore through the village at night and carried away the bridge that joined the farms to the market. By morning, people stood on opposite banks shouting instructions and accusations across the water.

Kofi recognized every argument. The carpenter blamed the builders. The builders blamed the council. The council blamed the weather. An old disagreement about land returned as if the flood had carried it back.

“We cannot rebuild while everyone is fighting,” Kofi said.

An elder lifted a length of chain from the mud. Several links were bent, but none had separated. “What do you see?”

“A chain.”

“I see pieces that remain themselves and still hold one another.”

The villagers did not become friends that morning. They began with smaller agreements. One group would gather timber. Another would prepare food. The strongest swimmers would inspect the riverbanks, while those who could not lift beams would count supplies, care for children, and keep tools dry.

Kofi watched a carpenter accept a measurement from a woman he had refused to speak with for years. Their hands met on the same board. Neither apologized yet. But neither let the bridge collapse because of the quarrel.

On the third day, the water rose again. A newly placed support slipped. People rushed toward it, each pulling in a different direction. Kofi called for everyone to stop.

“One voice at a time,” he said. “We can disagree about the method after we agree on what must be protected.”

They listened to the river expert, then the carpenter, then the farmer who knew where the ground stayed firm after rain. The answer came from all three.

By sunset the first person crossed the repaired bridge: a child carrying bread to her grandmother on the other bank. Nobody cheered for a particular family or trade. They cheered because someone had made it across.

The elder hung the recovered chain beside the bridge. “Unity is not pretending there are no broken places,” she said. “It is deciding that a broken place deserves our attention more than our pride.”

Kofi looked at the links. Each had its own shape, its own scratches, its own history. What made the chain useful was not sameness. It was connection.

The storm had shown the village how easily wood could break. The rebuilding showed them something harder to destroy: the choice to be responsible for one another.
