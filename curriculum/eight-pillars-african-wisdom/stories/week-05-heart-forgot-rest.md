## Story

In Accra, everyone knew Abena as the person who answered.

When a neighbor needed a ride, Abena answered. When a relative needed food, she answered. When someone called late at night, she answered before the second ring.

Her daughter Efua watched the way people smiled when Abena arrived. She also watched the way her mother sat alone after everyone left, too tired to finish her own supper.

One afternoon Abena forgot an appointment she had made for herself. She apologized to the people who needed her that evening, then rushed to help them anyway.

Auntie Esi found her standing in the kitchen, holding a cup of tea she had never taken time to drink.

“Who takes care of you?” Esi asked.

Abena gave a familiar answer. “I'm fine.”

Esi pulled out a chair. “That is not what I asked.”

For a long time Abena said nothing. She had confused being needed with being loved, and being useful with being worthy. She worried that saying no would make people think her heart had become small.

The next morning, a neighbor asked her to leave work early for an errand. Abena felt the old urgency rise. Then she said, “I can't do that today. I can help you find another way.”

The neighbor was disappointed. The sky did not fall.

Later, Efua asked whether her mother would sit with her in the courtyard. Abena nearly reached for her phone. Instead, she put it aside. They watched birds gather in the tree while the evening cooled around them.

Over the following weeks, Abena learned a different rhythm of care. She offered help when she could give it freely. She asked others for help when she needed it. She let people solve problems she had once rushed to carry for them.

One day she became ill and had to rest. Food arrived at the door. Efua brought water. Neighbors handled their own errands and checked on her without asking for anything.

Abena cried—not because she had become weak, but because she finally allowed herself to receive what she had offered for years.

Auntie Esi placed an Akoma symbol beside her bed. “A heart is not measured only by how much it gives away,” she said.

Abena held her daughter's hand. Compassion had not left her when she began setting boundaries. It had grown large enough to include herself.

And the people who loved her learned something too: a community becomes kinder when care is allowed to travel in both directions.
