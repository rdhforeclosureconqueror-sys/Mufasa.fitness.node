## Story

Adwoa was a young emergency-management engineer in the coastal city of Ahenkro. She knew how to read storm maps, test warning systems, and calculate how quickly floodwater might reach the lower streets.

She also knew what fear felt like.

When the first warning arrived, the sea looked almost peaceful. Some officials wanted to wait for more certainty before telling residents to prepare. Adwoa checked the measurements again. The storm had changed direction, and the low-lying neighborhoods had less time than anyone hoped.

She thought about the Akoben, the war horn: a call to vigilance and readiness.

Her hand trembled as she requested an emergency meeting.

“What if you're wrong?” someone asked.

“Then we will have prepared for a danger that did not arrive,” Adwoa said. “If I'm right and we wait, people may not have time to leave.”

The warning went out.

Some residents were angry. Others did not believe it. Adwoa did not answer fear with insults. She sent clear directions, asked local leaders to help, and listened when a wheelchair user explained that the published evacuation route was inaccessible. The team changed the route.

As the wind rose, Adwoa discovered that one shelter had lost power. She wanted to run there herself, but the operations room needed someone to coordinate the entire response. She called a trained team, confirmed their equipment, and stayed at her station.

Courage, she realized, was not always the dramatic act people could see. Sometimes it was staying responsible for the work only you could do.

The storm struck after dark. Water rushed through streets where children had played that morning. But buses had already carried families to higher ground, and the revised route helped people who might otherwise have been stranded.

At dawn, the city was damaged. Homes needed repair. People were frightened and tired. Adwoa walked past the shelter and saw neighbors sharing food, blankets, and news.

An older woman thanked her for being fearless.

Adwoa shook her head. “I was afraid the whole time.”

“Then what made you act?”

“I understood that the warning wasn't about me looking brave. It was about everyone having a chance to be ready.”

The horn had not promised safety or certainty. It had called people to pay attention and take responsibility while there was still time.

Adwoa looked toward the battered shoreline. She would keep learning, keep improving the warning system, and keep listening to the people it was meant to protect. That, she understood, was the work of courage.
