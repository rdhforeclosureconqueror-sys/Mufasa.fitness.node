Status: content contract for review; not deployed or medically validated.

## Core teaching: The Return
Customer preview: **Your mind wandering is not the end of meditation. Noticing it and gently coming back is the practice.**
Expanded: Choose one simple anchor, such as the feeling of breathing. Your attention will wander. When you notice, acknowledge it without judgment and return to your anchor. Repeat. This practices awareness and redirecting attention; it does not guarantee perfect focus or a particular outcome.
Steps: **Choose → Wander → Notice → Return → Repeat**.
Avoid: "you must fail," "meditation gives total thought control," or promises that attention improves by a fixed amount. Wandering is normal, not failure.

## What is meditation?
Customer preview: **Meditation is practice in noticing what your mind is doing and choosing where to place your attention.**
Expanded: Some meditation practices focus on breathing, sound, sensations, or a phrase; others cultivate open awareness. You do not have to empty your mind. In a sound bath, the changing tones can be an anchor to return to when your thoughts drift.

## What is pranayama?
Customer preview: **Pranayama is a traditional yoga practice of intentionally working with the breath.**
Expanded: The Sanskrit term is often interpreted through *prana* (life force or vital energy) and *ayama* (expansion, extension, or regulation). Traditional teachings about prana are spiritual concepts, not measurements of oxygen. Gentle breath awareness may help people regulate attention and experience calm. Avoid promising greater cell oxygenation or treating pranayama as a medical intervention.

## Your body's two response systems
Customer preview: **Your body has systems that help you mobilize for action and recover afterward.**
Expanded: The sympathetic branch of the autonomic nervous system helps mobilize the body for demands (often described as fight-or-flight). The parasympathetic branch supports rest, digestion, and recovery. Both are necessary and active in different proportions; neither is simply good or bad. Comfortable, slow breathing, sometimes with a slightly longer exhale, can support a calmer state for some people. Breathing is not an instant switch, and muscle tension is not always caused by stress.

## Three take-home practices (optional, not diagnostic)
1. **The 60-second Return:** Sit comfortably. Notice your natural breathing. When attention wanders, silently say "thinking" and return to the next comfortable breath. Repeat without judgment.
2. **Easy extended exhale:** For 1–2 minutes, inhale gently for a comfortable count (for example 3) and exhale for a slightly longer count (for example 4–5). Do not strain, force deep breaths, or hold your breath. Return to normal breathing if uncomfortable.
3. **One-point reset:** Name one thing you can feel (feet on floor), one thing you can hear, and one action you will take next. Bring your attention back if it wanders.

Stop any exercise that causes dizziness, chest discomfort, breathlessness, or distress; return to normal breathing. Adapt or seek individualized professional guidance for relevant respiratory/cardiovascular conditions. No breath retention or rapid hyperventilation in beginner customer content.
