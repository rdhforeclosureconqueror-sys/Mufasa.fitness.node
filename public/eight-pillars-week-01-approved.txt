THE VILLAGE OF SEVEN BRIDGES

The Seven Weights Kofi Left Behind

A Milele Fit African Wisdom Journey

Long ago, in a village surrounded by seven streams, lived a young bridge builder named Kofi.

Kofi was known throughout the village for his craftsmanship.

He could take wood, stone, and rope and create a bridge strong enough to carry families, farmers, and merchants safely across the water.

People admired his work.

But there was something Kofi struggled to build.

Relationships.

He was quick to anger when criticized.

Afraid to admit when he was wrong.

Proud of his accomplishments.

Troubled by what others thought of him.

And often so concerned with what he lacked that he forgot to appreciate what he already had.

One morning, after an argument with a fellow builder, Kofi walked toward the oldest part of the village.

There, beneath a great tree, sat an elder who had watched generations grow.

The elder noticed the frustration on Kofi’s face.

“You build bridges for everyone else,” the elder said, “yet you seem to carry walls wherever you go.”

Kofi frowned.

“I work hard. I do my best. But people always find something to criticize.”

The elder smiled gently.

“Then perhaps it is time you learn what makes a bridge truly strong.”

Beside the elder sat a leather satchel.

Inside were seven stones.

The elder handed it to Kofi.

“Carry this across the seven bridges before sunset.”

Kofi lifted the satchel.

It was surprisingly heavy.

“What are these stones for?”

“You will understand as you cross.”

“And what am I supposed to do with them?”

The elder looked toward the first bridge.

“Pay attention to what each crossing asks of you. When you understand what you have been carrying, you may leave one stone behind.”

Kofi sighed.

He had expected wisdom.

Instead, he had been given a bag of rocks.

But he began walking.

The First Bridge — Anger

As Kofi approached the first bridge, he encountered a man inspecting the wooden railing.

The man shook his head.

“So you’re the famous bridge builder? I’ve seen children build stronger things than this.”

Kofi stopped.

Heat rushed through his chest.

His jaw tightened.

He wanted to insult the man.

He wanted to remind him of every bridge he had built and every person who had crossed safely because of his work.

But as he reached for a response, he felt the satchel pulling against his shoulder.

He remembered the elder’s words.

Pay attention to what each crossing asks of you.

Kofi paused.

He took a slow breath.

Then another.

The anger was still there.

But he noticed something.

He could feel anger without giving it control of his words.

He looked at the man and said:

“You may question my work, but you will not decide how I treat you.”

The man looked surprised.

Kofi continued across the bridge.

Halfway over, he stopped and opened the satchel.

He understood the first weight.

It was not anger itself.

It was the belief that every insult required retaliation.

He placed one stone beside the path.

And the satchel became a little lighter.

The Second Bridge — Fear

At the second bridge, Kofi encountered a merchant preparing to cross with a cart of goods.

The merchant recognized him.

“Kofi! You helped build this crossing. Is it safe?”

Kofi looked closely at the wooden supports.

He recognized a section he had repaired months earlier.

But now one of the supports had shifted.

He remembered that he had rushed the repair.

The merchant waited for his answer.

Kofi felt fear rising.

If he admitted the mistake, people might question his skill.

They might stop trusting him.

He could simply say the bridge was safe.

Perhaps the cart would cross without trouble.

But then he imagined what could happen if the support failed.

He took a breath.

“I worked on this bridge,” Kofi said, “and I made a repair that needs to be corrected. I would not advise taking your cart across until it has been inspected and repaired.”

The merchant looked concerned.

“Then I will take the longer route.”

Kofi nodded.

“I will return to make sure this is addressed.”

The merchant thanked him for telling the truth.

As Kofi continued on foot along a safe path, he felt the satchel against his back.

He realized that his fear had not been protecting him.

It had been tempting him to protect his reputation at someone else’s expense.

He removed the second stone.

He left behind the fear of admitting his mistakes.

And the satchel grew lighter.

The Third Bridge — Ego

At the third bridge, Kofi found a younger builder working on a new support design.

The young man had discovered a clever way to strengthen the structure while using less wood.

Kofi studied it carefully.

It was an excellent idea.

Better than anything he had considered.

For a moment, his pride rose.

I am the master builder, he thought.

People expect me to have the best ideas.

He imagined taking the design to the village leaders and presenting it as his own.

Nobody would know.

But as he watched the young builder working, he remembered how much effort went into creating something new.

He also remembered how deeply he valued recognition for his own work.

Kofi approached him.

“This is impressive,” he said. “How did you develop it?”

The young builder’s face brightened.

He explained his design.

Kofi listened.

Then he said something that surprised even himself.

“You have discovered something I did not know. Would you teach me?”

The young builder smiled.

And together, they studied the support.

Kofi realized that learning from someone else did not diminish his abilities.

It expanded them.

He removed the third stone.

He left behind the ego that prevented him from learning.

And the satchel became lighter still.

The Fourth Bridge — Approval

As Kofi approached the fourth bridge, he heard villagers arguing near the road.

One insisted that Kofi was the finest builder in the region.

Another disagreed.

“There are better builders in the neighboring villages.”

Kofi slowed his steps.

He wanted to enter the conversation.

He wanted to defend his reputation.

He wanted everyone to agree that his work was exceptional.

But then he remembered the merchant at the second bridge.

There was still a damaged support that needed attention.

And the longer Kofi delayed, the longer the crossing would remain unsafe for heavy loads.

He looked toward the villagers.

They were still debating who deserved the most praise.

Then he turned around.

He returned to the damaged bridge.

He gathered the necessary tools and materials, arranged for help where needed, and began repairing the support.

Nobody was watching.

Nobody was praising him.

Nobody was arguing about whether he was the best.

The work was slow.

It required patience.

But Kofi continued until the support was properly repaired and the crossing could be checked for safety.

When he finished, he sat quietly beside the river.

He realized that the value of his work did not disappear when nobody applauded.

The bridge still served a purpose.

The repair still mattered.

And he had used his energy for something more meaningful than winning an argument.

Kofi removed the fourth stone.

He left behind the need for everyone’s approval.

And the satchel became lighter.

The Fifth Bridge — Comparison

Near the fifth bridge stood a beautiful home overlooking the river.

Its walls were carefully finished.

Its garden was filled with flowers.

Its courtyard was larger than Kofi’s entire property.

Kofi stopped to admire it.

Then admiration slowly turned into envy.

Why does this family have so much?

I work hard. I build things for everyone. Why don’t I have a home like this?

He looked down at his worn sandals.

He thought about the small house where he lived.

Suddenly, what had once felt sufficient seemed inadequate.

Then he noticed the owner tending the garden.

The man greeted him warmly.

“Kofi! Come and see the flowers. My family has spent years growing them.”

Kofi approached.

As they spoke, he learned how much patience and effort had gone into creating the garden.

The owner spoke proudly of the work his family had shared.

Kofi listened.

Then he thought about his own home.

The meals he had enjoyed there.

The laughter of his family.

The quiet evenings.

The memories that no larger house could replace.

He realized that another person’s blessings did not erase his own.

He congratulated the homeowner sincerely.

Then he continued walking.

At the edge of the bridge, he removed the fifth stone.

He left behind the burden of comparison.

And the satchel grew lighter.

The Sixth Bridge — Control

At the sixth bridge, Kofi found an elderly woman struggling to carry a bundle of firewood.

Without asking, he rushed forward.

“Here, let me take that.”

He reached for the bundle.

But the woman pulled it away.

“I did not ask you to take it.”

Kofi was startled.

“I was only trying to help!”

“And I appreciate your intention,” she replied. “But you did not ask what help I needed.”

Kofi felt irritation rising.

Why would someone refuse help?

Then he paused.

He remembered the first bridge.

He remembered the third.

And he recognized the familiar desire to have things done his way.

He looked at the woman.

“You are right,” he said. “What would be helpful to you?”

She smiled.

“My hands are tired, but I know how I want the wood arranged. Would you help me secure the bundle so I can carry it more comfortably?”

Kofi nodded.

Together, they adjusted the rope.

Then she asked whether he would accompany her across the bridge.

He gladly agreed.

As they walked, the woman told him stories about the village.

Kofi listened.

He realized that helping someone did not require taking control of their choices.

Sometimes the most respectful help began with a question.

At the far side of the bridge, he removed the sixth stone.

He left behind the need to control others.

And the satchel was nearly empty.

The Seventh Bridge — Perfection

The sun was beginning to set when Kofi reached the seventh bridge.

There, waiting beneath a tree, sat the elder.

Kofi smiled.

“I have crossed six bridges. I have learned to control my anger, tell the truth, set aside my ego, stop chasing approval, release comparison, and respect the choices of others.”

The elder nodded.

“And will you never feel anger again?”

Kofi hesitated.

“Of course I will.”

“Will you never make another mistake?”

“No.”

“Will your pride never return? Will you never compare yourself with another person?”

Kofi lowered his eyes.

“I suppose those feelings may return too.”

The elder looked toward the satchel.

“Then what have you learned?”

Kofi considered the question.

He had spent the entire day trying to become a better man.

Yet he could already imagine moments when he might fail.

Perhaps he would become angry again.

Perhaps he would seek approval.

Perhaps he would forget to listen.

For a moment, disappointment washed over him.

What if I cannot do all of this perfectly?

Then he remembered the six bridges.

At each one, he had encountered a challenge.

He had not always felt calm.

He had not always known the right thing to do immediately.

But he had learned to pause.

To recognize what was happening.

To choose.

And when necessary, to correct his course.

He looked at the elder.

“I think I understand,” he said.

“I do not have to become someone who never feels anger, fear, or pride.”

The elder listened.

“I have to become someone who recognizes those feelings and remembers that I can choose what to do with them.”

The elder smiled.

“And what happens when you forget?”

Kofi thought for a moment.

“Then I learn. I make amends where I need to. And I practice again.”

The elder nodded.

“The journey was never about becoming someone who never stumbles. It was about becoming someone who knows how to return.”

Kofi opened the satchel.

One stone remained.

It was the heaviest of them all.

And now he understood why.

He had been carrying the belief that he needed to become perfect before he could consider himself good.

He held the stone in his hands.

Then he placed it gently beside the seventh bridge.

He was not finished growing.

He would never be finished learning.

But he no longer needed perfection to begin living according to what he understood.

Like peppers growing on the same plant, people did not all develop at the same pace.

And even within one person, some lessons matured sooner than others.

Growth was not something he could force to happen all at once.

He could nurture it through patience, awareness, and practice.

The elder stood beside him.

“Tell me, Kofi. What did the seven bridges teach you?”

Kofi looked back toward the village.

He remembered the man who had insulted him.

The merchant who needed the truth.

The young builder whose wisdom he had nearly overlooked.

The villagers whose approval he had chased.

The beautiful home he had envied.

The woman whose choices he had almost ignored.

And finally, the impossible standard he had placed upon himself.

He smiled.

“I thought I was learning how to cross bridges.”

He paused.

“But I was learning how to stop becoming a wall.”

The elder looked toward the river.

“A bridge does not become strong by keeping people apart. Its strength is revealed by what it allows to connect.”

Kofi looked down at the empty satchel.

That morning, he had carried seven stones and believed they were the burden.

Now he understood.

The stones had only helped him recognize the weights he had been carrying within himself.

He had not changed the world in a single day.

He had changed the way he chose to move through it.

And that was where his journey began.

Kofi walked home beneath the evening sky.

His satchel was empty.

But his understanding was full.

The End.
